# Artisan metal studio: from idle capacity to a demand plan

## Snapshot
- **Business type:** one-person studio making handmade cast-metal art, sold through online marketplaces
- **Size:** single maker, small monthly sales
- **Region:** the Americas
- **Engagement:** two sessions plus asynchronous work, free pilot
- **Tools:** recorded interview and transcript, AI assistants (Claude), shared documents
- **Status:** plans drafted and under review; execution not started (see update log)

## Challenge
The owner wanted to multiply monthly sales by four within a year. The studio had spare production capacity but little marketing, very limited time for it, and several side product lines competing for attention.

## Approach
1. **Discovery interview** using seven blocks: product, customer, price, channels, goal and time frame, available time, and past attempts. It was recorded with permission and transcribed.
2. **Structured brief** from the transcript, separating what the owner said from our assumptions and from missing data.
3. **AI planning in plan mode**: the assistant first asks for missing data and proposes an approach, then writes. This avoided invented facts.
4. **Unit economics and funnel check** before any growth spend.
5. **Three plan versions** for different readers: a plain-language plan for the owner, a review version for a group of finance, production, sales and marketing professionals, and a second draft after two constraints were removed.
6. **Scenario comparison** of the constrained plan against a focused, time-balanced plan.

## What we found
1. **The constraint was demand, not capacity.** The studio could make more than it sold. A few hours a week of marketing could not create four times the buyers; a planned weekly block could.
2. **The stated margin did not reconcile.** When price is a multiple of the metal value, metal alone is 1 divided by the multiplier of the price. At the multipliers the owner described, metal explained a large share of price, so a very high stated margin could only hold at the top of the range or under a different cost definition. The first action was to verify costs per product, not to spend on growth.
3. **Side lines diluted the message and the hours.** Low-margin lines and a different customer type consumed time and sent a different message from the one aimed at the core buyer.
4. **Time needed a model, not good intentions.** A weekly template, quarterly shifts between production and marketing, a capacity formula (orders per month times hours per order, divided by 4.33) and rebalancing rules turned "find time for marketing" into a schedule.

## Decisions and trade-offs
A weighted decision matrix compared the two plans on seven criteria (expected revenue, downside protection, execution risk, time balance, brand coherence, contribution per founder hour, time to first results).

| Plan | Weighted score (1 to 5 scale) |
|---|---|
| Constrained: few marketing hours, side lines kept | 2.8 |
| Focused: one product line, balanced weekly time | 3.7 |

The focused plan costs more in the first quarter, because side income stops before new demand arrives, and recovers by roughly the middle of the year in the base scenario. The constrained plan would win if the side lines were a large share of cash flow, if the owner could not sustain the marketing hours, or if the side lines earned more per hour than the core product once real data arrived. Those conditions are listed in the plan so they can be checked.

## Deliverables (described, not shared)
- Discovery brief with tagged facts, assumptions and open data
- Data request message for the owner
- Session agenda
- 12-month plan for the owner (plain language)
- 12-month plan for a review group (assumptions, sensitivities, governance, risk register)
- Focused second draft with a comparison against the first
- Weekly scorecard, adjustment rules and a first-week day-by-day schedule

## Status
The plans are drafted and being reviewed with the owner. No sales outcome is claimed. Results will be added to the update log at 30, 90 and 180 days after execution begins.

## Lessons
- Ask about constraints early, then test whether they are real by removing one and rerunning the plan.
- Check unit economics before recommending spend.
- Write the same plan for each reader; a review group and an owner need different documents.
- Plan hours explicitly and give each block a rebalancing rule.
- Label every number as stated, estimated, assumed or pending.

## What I would do differently
Collect sales and cost data before the first plan draft, so the first version rests on records instead of estimates.
